Organisations reported 1,093 privacy breaches to the Privacy Commissioner in the 2024/25 year, up from 864 the year before. Many of those led to letters or emails telling people their details had been exposed. This guide helps you work out what a notice like that means for you, and what to do if your identity is being misused.

## You've received a breach notice

A notice should tell you what happened, what information was involved, what the organisation is doing, and what you can do. Read it for these specifics:

**What was exposed?** The answer decides your next step.

| If this was exposed | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| Email address only | Expect more phishing. Be extra careful with messages that mention the organisation. |
| A password, even scrambled | Change it, and change it anywhere else you used it. |
| Driver licence or passport number | Ask the organisation whether it will pay for a replacement. Many have. Consider a credit suppression. |
| Date of birth plus address | Consider a credit suppression. Watch for new accounts you didn't open. |
| Bank or card details | Call your bank and ask whether to replace the card. |
| Health or other sensitive information | Ask what was taken and whether it has been published. Ask what support is offered. |

**Ask questions.** If the notice is vague, write back and ask exactly which of your details were involved. You're entitled to ask an organisation for the information it holds about you, and our [request letter builder](/tools/request-letter/) can help.

**Be wary of follow-up messages.** Scammers send fake breach notices and fake "compensation" offers. Contact the organisation through its own website or app.

**If the notice came from a breach that harmed you**, you can complain to the organisation's privacy officer and then to the Privacy Commissioner.

## Credit file suppression {:#credit}

A credit file suppression, sometimes called a credit freeze, stops credit reporters releasing your credit information to lenders for a period. Since most lenders check your credit before giving credit, it makes it much harder for someone to borrow in your name.

The three credit reporters in New Zealand are **Centrix**, **Equifax**, and the business formerly known as **illion**, which now operates as Experian in New Zealand. Suppressions are governed by the Credit Reporting Privacy Code and are free.

- **One request can cover all three.** Centrix and Equifax can pass your request to the other reporters if you agree, so you don't have to apply three times.
- **The first suppression is short.** Centrix and Equifax say the initial suppression lasts 20 days. Set a reminder a few days before it ends.
- **You can ask to extend it** if you believe you are, or are likely to be, a victim of fraud. The reporter will consider the request, and the suppression continues while it does.
- **It doesn't affect existing credit.** Your cards and loans keep working. It may delay a new loan application, and you can ask for a temporary release when you genuinely apply for credit.
- **You'll need to prove who you are.** That protects you from someone suppressing, or lifting, a suppression on your file.

IDCARE, a free identity support service, keeps an up-to-date [guide to suppressions in New Zealand](https://idcare.org/fact-sheets/suppressions-new-zealand).

## Your ID has been stolen or is being misused

Work through these in order of risk:

1. **Passport:** report it lost or stolen to the Department of Internal Affairs so it's cancelled.
2. **Driver licence:** get a replacement through NZTA Waka Kotahi. A new licence has a new card version number, which helps against misuse of the old one.
3. **Credit:** place a suppression with the credit reporters.
4. **Bank:** tell your bank and ask it to add extra checks to your accounts.
5. **IRD:** tell Inland Revenue if your IRD number has been misused.
6. **Police:** report identity fraud on 105. You may need a Police reference number for banks and lenders.
7. **Support:** IDCARE (**0800 121 068**) will help you work out a response plan for your situation, free.

## Get a copy of your credit report

You can ask each credit reporter for your own credit report free. Look for enquiries or accounts you don't recognise. Do this once a year anyway, and straight away if you suspect misuse.

## Check what's already out there

The NCSC's [How Exposed Am I](https://www.howexposedami.co.nz/) checks your email addresses against known breaches. If one shows up, change the passwords linked to it.
